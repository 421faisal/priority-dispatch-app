What Is Freight Dispatching? A Practical Guide for Owner-Operators (2026)
By Muhammad Faisal Bilal, Founder & CEO, Priority Dispatch LLC · Last updated October 23, 2026
Freight dispatching is the work of finding, negotiating, booking and managing loads on behalf of a motor carrier. A dispatcher works for the carrier (the truck owner), not the shipper or the broker. A good one keeps the truck loaded on profitable lanes, checks that brokers will actually pay, and handles the paperwork so the driver can drive.
This guide explains who is involved in a load, what a dispatcher does day to day, what dispatching costs, how to check brokers before you book, and when you may not need a dispatcher at all. Where a number comes from a regulator or published source, it is linked at the end.
Who is involved in moving a load
PartyRoleWho pays whomShipperOwns the freight and needs it movedPays the broker (or the carrier directly)BrokerMatches the shipper's freight with a carrier; must hold FMCSA broker authorityPays the carrier, usually after delivery and paperworkCarrier / owner-operatorOwns the truck and holds the operating authority (MC number)Pays the dispatcher, if they use oneDispatcherFinds and books loads for the carrier, negotiates, handles paperworkIs paid by the carrierThe key distinction: a broker contracts with the shipper and pays the carrier. A dispatcher works under the carrier's authority and is paid by the carrier. Brokers must also keep a financial security of $75,000 (a BMC-84 surety bond or BMC-85 trust) on file with FMCSA. Dispatchers who work only for carriers do not.
What a freight dispatcher actually does
• Load search and booking. Searching load boards such as DAT and Truckstop, plus direct broker and shipper contacts, for freight that fits your equipment, schedule and home-time needs.
• Rate negotiation. Comparing the offered rate with recent rates on that lane and asking for more when the data supports it.
• Broker checks. Confirming the broker's authority is active, the bond is on file, and the payment record is acceptable before you commit.
• Route and lane planning. Looking beyond one load to what freight is available at the destination, so the truck is not stranded empty.
• Paperwork. Rate confirmations, carrier packets, bills of lading, invoices, and follow-up on detention, layover or TONU (truck order not used) charges.
• Problem solving. Delays, breakdowns, lumper issues and receiver disputes, ideally at any hour.
What a dispatcher cannot do for you
Your authority, insurance and safety record are yours. A dispatcher cannot replace them:
• Operating authority. For-hire interstate carriers need an MC number from FMCSA.
• Insurance. FMCSA requires proof of liability insurance on file before authority is granted. For general freight the minimum is $750,000, and some cargo types require more, up to $5,000,000.
• A BOC-3 process agent filing, which every carrier with operating authority must have on file.
• Safe, compliant driving. Dispatch can plan a route, but hours-of-service and vehicle condition remain the driver's and carrier's responsibility.
You also stay the one who signs the rate confirmation. Read it yourself, and never haul without a signed one.
The life of a load, step by step
1. Search. The dispatcher filters loads by equipment, origin, destination and minimum rate.
2. Vet the broker. Check authority, bond and payment history (see the checklist below).
3. Negotiate. Ask for a rate that reflects the lane, the deadhead to pick up, and any extra stops or tight appointment times.
4. Confirm. Review the rate confirmation for the rate, pickup and delivery windows, detention terms, and who pays for lumper fees. Get it signed before moving.
5. Dispatch and track. Send the driver the pickup details and monitor progress.
6. Deliver and document. Collect the signed proof of delivery and BOL.
7. Invoice and collect. Submit the invoice, or send it to a factoring company, and follow up until it is paid.
8. Plan the next load before the current one delivers.
Why lane planning matters: a worked example
The highest rate on a single load is not always the best deal. What counts is the all-in rate per mile, which means total revenue divided by every mile driven, loaded and empty. These numbers are illustrative, not market quotes.
Option A: A 1,000-mile load pays $3.00 per mile, which is $3,000. The destination is a weak freight market, so the driver deadheads 400 miles to the next pickup. Revenue is $3,000 over 1,400 total miles, which is about $2.14 per mile.
Option B: A 600-mile load at $2.60 per mile pays $1,560. It delivers into a stronger market, and a 800-mile load at $2.45 per mile follows, paying $1,960. Revenue is $3,520 over 1,400 total miles, which is about $2.51 per mile.
Option B has a lower rate on every loaded leg but earns about $520 more over the same distance, because no miles are empty. Checking the next load before accepting the current one is a large part of a dispatcher's value.
What dispatching costs
Most dispatch services charge a percentage of each load's revenue. Industry guides commonly cite a range of 5% to 10%, and some services charge a flat weekly or per-load fee instead. On $10,000 of weekly gross revenue:
FeeCost per week5%$5007%$70010%$1,000When comparing services, look beyond the headline percentage:
• Is the fee calculated on linehaul only or on gross including fuel surcharge?
• Is there a long contract or minimum term, or can you leave with short notice?
• Is there a forced-dispatch rule, or can you decline loads?
• Will you see every rate confirmation and the rate the broker actually offered?
• Are there setup fees, software fees or other charges?
A lower percentage is not automatically cheaper. A dispatcher who finds loads that pay ten cents per mile more can cost less in the end than a cheaper one who does not. Compare your net revenue per truck over several weeks.
How to vet a broker before you book
Freight fraud is a real risk for carriers. Double brokering happens when a load is accepted and then secretly reassigned to another carrier, and the carrier doing the work may never be paid. Carrier identity theft, where scammers clone a legitimate carrier's USDOT or MC details, is another common scheme. FreightWaves has cited estimates of annual double-brokering losses of $500 million to $700 million, though such figures are estimates and vary by source.
A practical checklist before you accept a load:
1. Verify the authority. Look up the broker's MC or USDOT number in FMCSA's SAFER system or the Licensing & Insurance site. Confirm the status is active and the company name, phone number and address match the contact you were given.
2. Check the bond. Brokers must keep $75,000 of financial security on file. Under FMCSA's financial responsibility rule, in effect since January 16, 2026, a broker whose security drops below that amount must restore it within a short window or risk suspension of authority.
3. Review payment history. Use a carrier credit-reporting service or the broker's reviews from other carriers, and set your own limit for days-to-pay.
4. Check whether the invoice can be factored. If your factoring company will not approve the broker's credit, treat that as a warning.
5. Watch for warning signs. These include pressure to skip checks, a recently changed phone number or email, a very new authority offering a very high volume of freight, or requests for advance payment or credentials outside normal onboarding.
6. Get the rate confirmation in writing, signed by both sides, before the truck moves.
7. Report suspected fraud to the load board and to FMCSA, and keep all documentation.
No checklist removes all risk. Keep your own records of every load, including the rate confirmation, emails and proof of delivery.
Do you need a dispatcher?
A dispatcher is not right for everyone.
A dispatcher tends to help when:
• You are new, with a new MC number and no broker relationships yet.
• You spend hours a week on the phone, load boards and paperwork instead of driving or resting.
• You run several trucks and need someone watching all of them.
• Your lanes are in or out of weak markets where planning the next load matters.
You may not need one when:
• You already have direct shipper contracts or dependable broker relationships that keep the truck full at good rates.
• You prefer to negotiate and enjoy it, and have time for the admin.
• The fee would take a large share of a thin margin on low-paying freight.
A fair test is to track your rate per mile, deadhead percentage and weekly gross for four weeks before and after you start, and judge the service on those numbers.

Frequently asked questions
What is freight dispatching in simple terms? It is finding and booking loads for a trucking company, negotiating rates, checking brokers, and handling the paperwork around each load.
What is the difference between a dispatcher and a freight broker? A broker contracts with the shipper and pays the carrier, and must hold FMCSA broker authority and a $75,000 bond or trust. A dispatcher works for the carrier and is paid by the carrier.
How much does a truck dispatcher charge? Commonly 5% to 10% of each load's revenue, though flat weekly and per-load pricing exists. Confirm whether the percentage is calculated on linehaul only or on gross.
Do I need my own MC number to use a dispatcher? If you haul regulated freight for hire across state lines, yes. You need your own operating authority, insurance filings and BOC-3 on file.
Can a dispatcher guarantee higher rates? No. A dispatcher can improve your odds with lane data, negotiation and load planning, but rates depend on the market. Be cautious of any service that guarantees a specific result.
